The Best Seat for a Nervous Flyer

A 5-minute read

Where you sit can change how a flight feels. It won't change how safe the plane is, but it can make bumps feel gentler, the engines sound quieter and the whole trip feel more in your control. Here's how to pick the seat that suits you best.

Over the wing: the smoothest ride

If turbulence is your main worry, aim for a seat over the wing. The wings are close to the plane's centre of balance, so the middle of the plane moves the least when it bumps. Think of a seesaw. The middle barely moves while the ends go up and down.

Seats near the back usually feel the most motion. Seats near the front are often in between. On most planes, you can find the wing on the airline's seat map. It's usually marked, or you can look for the over-wing exits.

Window or aisle?

There's no right answer here. It depends on what calms you.

A window seat might suit you if:

  • Seeing outside helps you make sense of the movement. Watching the horizon can ease the feeling of motion for some people.
  • You like being able to see the wing working normally, the flaps moving and the ground slowly getting closer on landing.
  • You like a wall to lean on and a small space of your own.

An aisle seat might suit you if:

  • Feeling trapped is part of your anxiety. Being able to stand up without asking anyone can make a big difference.
  • You'd rather not see out at all.
  • You want easy access to the crew, the toilet or your bag.

If you're not sure, think about where you sit in a cinema or on a train. That's often a good clue.

Front or back?

  • Towards the front, it's often quieter, because on most airliners the engines are on the wings behind you. You'll also get off sooner, which helps if you find the end of the flight hard.
  • Towards the back, you'll usually feel more motion and hear more engine noise. Some people like being near the rear galley, where the crew spend much of their time.

No seat is reliably safer than another, so choose for comfort, not for safety. That choice is yours to make.

What about the exit row?

Exit rows have extra legroom, which is tempting. But if you sit there, you're agreeing to help open the exit and assist others in an emergency, and the crew will brief you on it. For some nervous flyers, that briefing makes things worse. For others, having a clear role feels steadying. Be honest with yourself about which you are. You can always ask to move if you change your mind.

Bulkhead seats, at the front of a cabin section, also have more room, without the exit duties. They're a good alternative.

Bigger planes often feel steadier

If you have a choice of flights, a larger aircraft tends to feel steadier in bumpy air than a small regional one. Both are safe. It's just that a heavier plane is nudged around less by the same pocket of air. Your booking or the airline's website will usually tell you the aircraft type.

How to get the seat you want

  • Choose early. Seats over the wing and near the front go quickly. Picking at booking, or as soon as seat selection opens, gives you the best chance.
  • Check the seat map. Look for the wing and the exits, and avoid seats right by the toilets if queues bother you.
  • Ask at check-in or the gate. Explain kindly that you're a nervous flyer. Staff can't always help, but they often try.
  • Mention it on board. If there are free seats over the wing, a flight attendant may be able to move you after take-off.

Remember, the best seat is a help, not a requirement. Wherever you end up, the plane is built for the journey, and your breathing and calming skills come with you.

Common questions

Where on a plane do you feel turbulence the least?

Seats over the wing usually feel the least motion, because they are close to the plane's centre of balance. Seats near the back usually feel the most.

Is a window or aisle seat better for anxiety?

It depends on you. A window can help if seeing outside makes the movement easier to understand. An aisle can help if feeling trapped is part of your anxiety, because you can stand up without asking anyone.

Is any seat on a plane safer than another?

No seat is reliably safer than another, so it makes sense to choose for comfort. Wearing your seatbelt whenever you are seated matters far more than where you sit.

Should a nervous flyer sit in the exit row?

Only if you are comfortable with the role. Exit row passengers agree to help open the exit in an emergency and the crew will brief them on it. A bulkhead seat offers extra room without those duties.
