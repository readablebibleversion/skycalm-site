How to Calm a Panic Attack on a Plane

A 5-minute read

A panic attack can feel huge anywhere. On a plane, where you can't step outside, it can feel even bigger. If it happens to you, you're not weak and you're not alone. Panic is uncomfortable, but it passes. Here's what you can do in the moment, step by step.

SkyCalm is a wellness app, not medical advice. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing that doesn't ease, or you're not sure it's panic, tell a flight attendant straight away.

What's happening in your body

A panic attack is your body's alarm system going off when there's no fire. Adrenaline floods in. Your heart races, your breathing speeds up, and you might feel shaky, hot, dizzy or sick. Your mind may tell you something terrible is about to happen.

These feelings are frightening, but they're your body trying to protect you. The surge peaks and then fades, usually within minutes. You don't have to make it stop. You only have to ride it out, and the steps below make that easier.

Step 1: Slow your breathing

Breathing is the quickest way to tell your body it's safe. The key is a longer breath out than in.

  • Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of 4.
  • Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of 6, as if blowing on hot soup.
  • Keep going for a few minutes. Don't worry about getting it perfect.

If counting is hard, just make each breath out a little slower than the one before. Rest a hand on your belly and feel it rise and fall.

Step 2: Ground yourself with 5-4-3-2-1

Panic pulls your attention into worst-case thoughts. Grounding brings it back to the seat you're sitting in. Name, silently or out loud:

  • 5 things you can see. The seat pocket, a reading light, the pattern on the seat in front.
  • 4 things you can feel. Your feet on the floor, the armrest, your sleeve, the seatbelt.
  • 3 things you can hear. The hum of the engines, a trolley, someone talking.
  • 2 things you can smell. Coffee, your own jumper, hand cream.
  • 1 thing you can taste. A sip of water or a mint works well here.

It sounds simple. That's the point. It gives your busy mind a small, doable job.

Step 3: Use your body

  • Press your feet into the floor and notice the floor pressing back.
  • Squeeze and release. Clench your fists or the armrests for five seconds, then let go and feel the difference.
  • Cool down. Turn the air vent towards your face, or hold a cold drink.
  • Drop your shoulders and unclench your jaw. Tension often hides there.

Step 4: Tell the crew

You're allowed to ask for help. Flight attendants are trained to look after nervous flyers, and many will tell you it's one of the most common things they deal with. Press the call button or catch someone's eye and say something simple, like:

"I'm a nervous flyer and I'm having a panic attack. I'm okay, I just wanted you to know."

They can bring water, check in on you, explain a noise or a bump, or simply stand with you for a minute. Telling someone often takes a lot of the pressure off on its own. If you'd rather not speak, you can show them a short note on your phone.

What makes it worse

  • Fighting it. Telling yourself "stop panicking" tends to add a second layer of fear. Try "this is panic, it will pass" instead.
  • Lots of caffeine. It can make your heart race, which feels like anxiety.
  • Alcohol. It may seem to help at first, but it can leave you more anxious later and dehydrated.
  • Checking and scanning. Staring at the wing or the crew for signs of trouble keeps your alarm switched on.

Afterwards

When it passes, you might feel tired or a bit shaky. That's normal. Drink some water, and give yourself credit. You just got through one of the hardest moments a nervous flyer can have.

If panic attacks happen often, or fear of flying is holding you back, it's worth talking to your doctor or a therapist. Fear of flying is very treatable, and you don't have to manage it on your own.

Common questions

Can I tell the flight attendant I am having a panic attack?

Yes. Flight attendants are trained to help nervous flyers. A simple sentence like "I am a nervous flyer and I am having a panic attack" is enough. They can bring water, explain what is happening and check in on you.

How long does a panic attack last?

The surge of a panic attack usually peaks and then fades within minutes. Slow breathing and grounding can help you ride it out. If symptoms do not ease, or you are not sure it is panic, tell the crew straight away.

What is the 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique?

Name 5 things you can see, 4 you can feel, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell and 1 you can taste. It brings your attention back to the present moment and away from worst-case thoughts.

Should I take medication for a panic attack on a plane?

That is a question for your doctor, ideally well before your trip. Avoid trying any new medication for the first time on a flight.
