Plane Noises Explained: What's That Sound?

A 6-minute read

Planes are noisy, and most of the noises are never explained to you. A sudden thud or a strange whine can set your heart racing, simply because you don't know what it is. Here are the sounds you're most likely to hear, in the order you'll hear them. Every one of them is normal.

At the gate

A steady hum or roar from the back. Before the engines start, many planes run a small extra engine in the tail called the APU, or auxiliary power unit. It powers the lights and air conditioning while you board. You may also hear the air conditioning itself as a constant rush of air from the vents.

Thumps and clunks under the floor. That's bags being loaded into the hold and the cargo doors closing.

The "barking dog". On Airbus A320-family planes, you may hear a loud, repeating bark or growl from under the floor, often during taxi or while the cargo doors move. It's a pump called the power transfer unit, or PTU. The plane has separate hydraulic systems, and the PTU uses one to help keep the pressure up in another. It's a sign of a system doing exactly what it was designed to do. It sounds odd, but it's completely routine.

Engine start and taxi

A rising whine. Each engine spins up slowly, with a high-pitched whine that grows into a steady hum. Engines are usually started one at a time.

Lights or screens flickering. As the engines take over from the APU or ground power, the plane switches its power source. Cabin lights and screens can blink for a moment. That's normal.

Bumps and rattles on the ground. Taxiways have joints and lights set into them, and the wheels roll over them. Overhead bins and trolleys rattle too. A plane on the ground often feels bumpier than one in the air.

A squeal or squeak. The brakes, used gently as the plane taxis and turns.

Take-off and climb

The big roar. The engines go to take-off power and the noise builds quickly. The rumble you feel is the wheels rolling fast on the runway.

A thud and a whir just after lift-off. That's the landing gear folding up into the plane and the gear doors closing behind it. It can be surprisingly loud and you may feel it through the floor.

The engines go quieter. A minute or two after take-off, the pilots reduce power from take-off thrust to climb thrust. It can feel as if the plane is slowing or sinking. It isn't. The plane keeps climbing, just more gently. This is one of the sounds that worries people most, and it happens on almost every flight.

A humming whine from the wings. The flaps, which are extra wing surfaces used for take-off, are being tucked away as the plane speeds up. Electric motors drive them, so you'll hear a whir for several seconds at a time.

In the cruise

Chimes. Planes chime for lots of reasons: the seatbelt sign turning on or off, a passenger pressing the call button, or the pilots and cabin crew calling each other on the interphone. The exact tones differ between airlines and aircraft. A chime is a doorbell, not an alarm.

Changes in engine sound. Small changes in pitch are normal as the pilots or autopilot adjust speed and altitude.

Whooshes from the toilets. Plane toilets use a vacuum system, which is why the flush is so loud.

Descent and approach

Quieter engines again. To start coming down, the engines are often set close to idle. The plane glides down gradually, so the cabin can feel very quiet.

A rumble or shudder from the wings. Pilots may raise the spoilers, also called speed brakes. These panels on top of the wing lift up to add drag and help the plane slow down or descend. You'll see them if you're by the window, and you may feel a light vibration.

Flaps again. The same whir as after take-off, this time as the flaps come out in stages to let the plane fly slowly and safely for landing.

A deep rumble and a clunk. The landing gear coming down and locking into place. The extra wind noise afterwards is the air rushing past the wheels.

Your ears popping. The cabin pressure changes as you descend. Swallowing, yawning or chewing helps.

Landing

A thump. The wheels meeting the runway. Some landings are firm on purpose, especially on wet runways, because a firm touchdown helps the tyres grip.

A loud roar right after touchdown. That's reverse thrust. The engines redirect their air forwards to help slow the plane. It sounds like the plane is speeding up, but it's doing the opposite.

The spoilers pop up. On landing, the wing panels rise fully to press the plane onto the runway and help the brakes work.

Once you know these sounds, they start to sound like what they are: a busy machine working normally. Each one is a sign that something is going to plan.

Common questions

What is the barking dog sound on an Airbus?

It is the power transfer unit, or PTU, a pump that lets one hydraulic system help keep pressure up in another. On Airbus A320-family aircraft it is often heard during taxi or when the cargo doors move. It is a normal, routine sound.

Why do the engines go quiet after take-off?

A minute or two after take-off, the pilots reduce power from take-off thrust to climb thrust. It can feel like the plane is slowing or sinking, but it keeps climbing, just more gently. It happens on almost every flight.

What is the loud thud after take-off?

That is the landing gear folding into the plane and the gear doors closing behind it. You often feel it through the floor shortly after lift-off.

What do the chimes on a plane mean?

Chimes signal routine things like the seatbelt sign changing, a passenger call button or the crew calling each other. The exact tones vary by airline and aircraft. They are not alarms.
