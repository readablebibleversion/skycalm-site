Turbulence Forecasts: How to Check Your Route Before You Fly

A 5-minute read

Not knowing is often the hardest part. If you'd rather know whether your flight is likely to be smooth or bumpy, you can check. Turbulence forecasts are used by airlines every day, and simple versions are available to you too. Here's how to find one, how to read it, and how to use it without letting it take over your week.

What a turbulence forecast is

Weather services run computer models of the atmosphere. From them, they can estimate where the air is likely to be choppy. They look at things like wind speed changes with height, the position of the jet stream, mountain ranges and storms.

The result is a map of where bumps are more or less likely, at different heights and times. It's a forecast, like a rain forecast. It tells you about chances, not certainties.

Where to check

  • A flight-focused app or site. Some tools let you enter a flight number or a route and show the outlook along the way. SkyCalm's flight plan does this for your route. There are also public websites, such as Turbli, that show turbulence forecasts for flights.
  • Aviation weather services. In the US, the National Weather Service's Aviation Weather Center publishes the same kind of turbulence guidance that pilots and dispatchers use. It's detailed and technical, so a simpler tool is usually easier for nervous flyers.
  • Your crew. On the day, the pilots have the latest forecasts and reports from planes ahead. They'll often mention expected bumps in their welcome announcement. You can also ask a flight attendant.

How to read it

Turbulence is usually described in a few levels. Here's roughly what each one feels like from your seat:

  • Light. The most common. Gentle bumps, like a car on an uneven road. Drinks ripple. You can walk around, though it may be a bit wobbly.
  • Moderate. Firmer bumps. Walking is difficult, and the crew may sit down and pause the service. Uncomfortable, but the plane handles it easily.
  • Severe. Rare for passengers to experience. Sharp jolts that can throw unbelted people around. Pilots work hard to avoid these areas, which is one of the reasons they study forecasts.

Many tools also show when on your route the bumps are likely, for example during the climb, over a mountain range or near the end. That's useful. If you know the second hour might be bumpy, you can plan your toilet trip and settle in with your seatbelt fastened before it starts.

What forecasts can't tell you

Forecasts are good, but they aren't perfect. A smooth forecast doesn't guarantee a smooth flight, and a bumpy one often turns out milder than expected. Clear-air turbulence, which happens in cloudless skies, is especially hard to pin down.

That's fine, because the forecast isn't your safety net. The plane is built for turbulence, the pilots are trained for it, and your seatbelt protects you from the one real risk. The forecast is just a way to set your expectations.

What pilots do with the same information

Airlines plan routes and heights with turbulence in mind. In the air, pilots share reports with each other and with air traffic control. If one altitude is bumpy, they'll often ask to move to a smoother one. They switch on the seatbelt sign early when they expect bumps, and they slow down to a speed chosen for rough air. Seeing a forecast for your route means you're looking at a small slice of what your crew is already managing.

Using forecasts without overdoing it

Checking can calm you. Checking again and again can feed the worry. A simple routine works well for most people:

  • Look once the day before to get a feel for the flight.
  • Look once on the day, as forecasts get more accurate closer to departure.
  • Then stop. Make a plan for any bumpy part and leave the rest to the crew.

If the forecast shows bumps, prepare rather than dread. Pick a seat over the wing if you can, have your breathing ready, and remind yourself what light and moderate feel like. A bumpy forecast is a reason to buckle up, not a reason to cancel.

Common questions

Can you check turbulence before a flight?

Yes. Turbulence forecasts are published every day for aviation, and some apps and websites show the outlook along a specific route or flight. They show where bumps are more or less likely, not a guarantee.

How accurate are turbulence forecasts?

They are a useful guide, but not perfect. A smooth forecast does not guarantee a smooth flight, and a bumpy forecast often turns out milder. Clear-air turbulence is especially hard to predict, which is why it is wise to keep your seatbelt fastened whenever you are seated.

What does moderate turbulence feel like?

Firmer bumps that make walking difficult. The crew may sit down and pause the service. It is uncomfortable, but airliners handle it easily.

Should I change my flight if turbulence is forecast?

Usually there is no need. Planes are built for turbulence and pilots plan around it. A bumpy forecast is a reason to prepare: keep your seatbelt on, choose a seat over the wing if you can and have your breathing ready.
